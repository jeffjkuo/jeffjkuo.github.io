Personal Statement

I am an Assistant Research Fellow at the National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology in Kaohsiung, Taiwan. My research lies at the intersection of applied econometrics, international trade, and political economy, with a particular focus on the use of quantitative methods to examine policy-relevant economic questions in open economies.

My academic and professional development has been shaped by research experience in both Taiwan and the United States, as well as by engagement with policy analysis, consulting, and scholarly collaboration. Over the course of this work, I have benefited from affiliations with and training at institutions including The George Washington University, Syracuse University, Georgetown University, Purdue University, Temple University, and the Chung-Hua Institution for Economic Research. I also strongly believe that writing and communicating in English remains essential in academic life, including in Taiwan, because it provides one of the most effective means of exchanging ideas and of engaging quickly with new topics, methodologies, and technologies.

Prior to my doctoral training in Washington, D.C., I earned a master's degree in Economics from Syracuse University. I previously graduated with honors from National Chengchi University in Taipei, where I completed a combined BS/MS degree in International Business. This academic background has informed my longstanding interest in the interaction among economics, public policy, and global exchange.

My primary objective is to produce empirically rigorous research that advances understanding of trade agreements, globalization, and related policy questions, with particular attention to Taiwan's position in the global economy. I am especially interested in combining econometric analysis, computational methods, and policy-oriented inquiry to study how regional integration, firm behavior, and political-economic adjustment interact. I am currently working on projects involving input-output table analysis and the revision of Taiwan's food balance sheet, both of which reflect my broader interest in connecting empirical measurement with policy-relevant economic analysis. This orientation is also reflected in my latest publication, which comments on and analyzes the potential economic impact on Taiwan following the 2026 meeting between Donald J. Trump and Xi Jinping in Beijing. More broadly, I aim to develop a research agenda that contributes to academic debates while remaining relevant to policy audiences and institutional decision-making.
