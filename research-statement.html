Research Statement

My research examines trade-related topics, especially the impact of trade policy and the dynamics of trade negotiations, at the country, industry, firm, product, or even transactional level. I work across building applied econometric models, computational GE methods, input-output analysis, and various datasets in Taiwan to assess the impact of trade agreements. I also served as a policy consultant on the Taiwanese negotiating delegation twice, for 21UTTI and EPPD in Washington, D.C., and organized multiple inter-institutional meetings between think tanks and government institutions between Taiwan and the U.S.

International Trade has been the oldest topic in economics, and some might argue it’s the beginning of modern economics. I have a special interest in using economic models and data to interpret the impacts of preferential trade agreements, the behavior of multinational firms under globalization, and the broader interaction among trade, technological change, and geopolitical adjustment. Currently, I am part of the research team responsible for improving Taiwan's food balance sheet by cross-referencing the UN-FAO framework and assessing the industrial trends of manufacturing sectors in Kaohsiung City.

Another central thread in my work studies how institutional reform can generate both aggregate gains and social frictions. Since I witnessed Taiwan's economic transformation under export-oriented development, I have focused on how growth, distribution, and policy adjustment interact in open economies. In a project I co-worked on with Professor Stacey H. Chen at the Institute of Economics at Academia Sinica, where we revisited wage rigidity in Taiwan's labor market and found that public narratives about the expansion of universities in Taiwan as the main culprit of wage stagnation are clearly incorrect given the obvious timing of policy ramifications; we show our argument with clearer empirical evidence. That short monograph became widely cited in Taiwan’s economic circle.

2026 is an anti-hyperglobalization era; how can we deal with multilateralism and integration, which often improve efficiency but may also intensify inequality and political backlash? This perspective also informs my latest publication, which comments on and analyzes the potential economic impact on Taiwan following the 2026 meeting between Donald J. Trump and Xi Jinping in Beijing. My goal is to identify these mechanisms by estimating empirically grounded models and by developing theoretical frameworks that characterize political-economic equilibria in open economies.
